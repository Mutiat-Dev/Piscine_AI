# Understand how roles affect the model’s perspective.

    Write a prompt asking the same question (e.g., "What is climate change?").

# Model output

# As a scientist, climate change is defined as:

    A long-term alteration in Earth’s average temperature and climate patterns, primarily driven today by increased greenhouse gases from human activities.

    It involves sustained changes in:
- Global temperature
- Precipitation patterns
- Sea levels
- Frequency of extreme weather

    The main cause is the enhanced greenhouse effect due to emissions from burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and industry.

# As a journalist speaking to the public:

    Climate change is the long-term change in Earth’s weather patterns, especially the steady rise in global temperatures, largely caused by human activities like burning fossil fuels and cutting down forests.

    In everyday terms, it means:
- Hotter average temperatures
- More frequent heatwaves
- Stronger storms and floods
- Rising sea levels
- Changing rainfall patterns

    Unlike daily weather changes, climate change refers to shifts happening over decades. Scientists say these changes are mainly driven by greenhouse gases that trap heat in the atmosphere.

As a news communicator, the key point is this: climate change affects food, water, health, jobs, and communities worldwide — making it both an environmental and a societal issue.

# As a critic, 

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in global climate patterns, widely attributed to human-driven greenhouse gas emissions. The scientific consensus is strong. However, criticism often focuses on several areas:

1️⃣ Political Framing
Climate change is frequently used as a political tool. Policies proposed in its name can reflect ideological goals, economic restructuring, or geopolitical interests. Critics ask whether every proposed solution is proportionate, effective, or evidence-based.

2️⃣ Economic Trade-offs
Rapid transitions away from fossil fuels can significantly affect developing economies, energy prices, and employment. Critics question whether climate policies fairly consider countries that rely heavily on oil, gas, or coal for development.

3️⃣ Media Narratives
Some argue that media coverage can amplify worst-case scenarios without sufficient nuance, potentially creating fear rather than informed public understanding. Others argue the opposite—that urgency is underreported. A critic examines whether coverage is balanced and evidence-driven.

4️⃣ Policy Effectiveness
Are carbon taxes, bans, and regulations actually reducing emissions at the scale required? Or are they symbolic gestures? Critics evaluate measurable outcomes rather than intentions.

5️⃣ Equity and Responsibility
Developed nations historically emitted more greenhouse gases. Critics question whether current climate agreements fairly distribute responsibility and costs between industrialized and developing countries.

# Relection

The first talks about the science behind climate change, the technicalities involved and the main causes. As a journalist, the output makes a key point to talk about how climate change affects food, water, health, jobs, and communities worldwide, making it both an environmental and a societal issue. A critic views climate change as both a scientific issue and a complex political, economic, and social debate.
